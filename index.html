SOLIDS

Solids is a robust, open-source Python package for crystal structure prediction. It natively integrates with the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) and uses PyXtal and DScribe to generate symmetry-based random structures and compare their structural features using morphological descriptors.

  • It is available in the official Python Package Index repository and can be easily installed using pip.
  • It can be run directly in cloud computing environments, such as Google Colab or Kaggle
  • It is compatible with UNIX systems—such as Linux and macOS
  • It offers full integration with GULP, VASP, and EMT
  • It can run as a Python library within resource management systems such as PBS or SLURM, making it well-suited for use on computing clusters.

Solids performs energy landscape exploration through two main algorithms:

  • The Stochastic Algorithm allows for effective pree-screening of the energy landscape by performing relaxation in stages, meaning that the level of theory can be adjusted or changed mid-run.
  • The Evolutionary Algorithm builds on SA by creating new generations using evolutive operators such as crossover and mutation to deepend the exploration of the potential energy surface.

Solids’ Workflow

A little bit of theory

Crystalline Structures and their Relationship with Energy

It is no secret that using modern first principle calculations and the knowledge of the atomic arrangement of a system, we can estimate and even predict its properties. Also, we know that theoretical low-energy structures have the highest probabilities of being found in nature. Therefore, locating the nuclear positions of a chemical system when it is lowest in energy, i.e., the ground state equilibrium geometry, is incredibly important to modern chemists. If we add to this argument that such theoretical investigations allow one to analyze structures under extreme conditions of pressure and temperature, this makes them an excellent ally to cumbersome studies.

However, in order to locate the structural geometry lowest in energy, the so-called Global Minimum (GM), is not a trivial task. Any algorithm tasked with the finding of a GM is faced with two major challenges: The first one is of mathematical nature since the dimension of the abstract construct —known as the Potential Energy Surface (PES)— where we plot each possible atomic configuration vs its energy, depends on the degrees of freedom of the system (3N-6 for a non-linear molecule with N atoms). This means that the PES is exceedingly multidimensional!

The second challenge is of statistical nature, since there is an almost infinite number of possible arrangements for a given set of atoms. Actually, it is estimated that the number of possible configurations for a periodic system with a cubic cell volume of 10 Å3 containing only 10 atoms of compound AB, is of the order of 1014. If we increase the number of atoms inside the unit cell up to 30, possible configurations increase up to a staggering 1047. Imagine having to evaluate the energy of all these configurations, compare them, and then locate the one with the lowest energy of them all. This is, to say it mildly, a really impractical approach

So, if we wish to locate the atomic configuration with the lowest possible energy, a.k.a the GM, of a chemical composition, we need a way to intelligently explore some of the most promising configurations that the complicated PES has to offer. Our tool has to be capable of locating with reasonable accuracy, in reasonable time, and using reasonable resources, the GM of any given chemical composition, under any condition of pressure and temperature. Seems impossible?

Evolutive Algorithms to the Rescue

There are many proposals as to how to efficiently explore the PES and locate GMs for all kinds of chemical compositions. However, there is a specific set of algorithms —whose official title is Evolutive Algorithms, or EAs— that generate a collection of random initial candidates and refine their characteristics, minimizing the energy associated with their atomic arrangement through a combination of local optimization means and evolutive operators. EAs mimic the evolution of biological species where desirable traits are passed on through generations until an optimal set of individuals are created.

Crossover

In the context of crystalline structures, we refer to “desirable traits” to energetically stable motifs of the crystal. These chracteristics have to be inherited to new structures aiming to reduce the enegy. To do so, one selects two low-energy “parents” and “cuts” them across the same unit cell vector. Then, one matches the two parent-slices to create a “child” that retains their structural information. The idea is that, across time, the good characteristics of the structures are refined. The process in charge of this is known as Crossover and a graphic representation is found in the image below. Notice that both parents pass on only a segment of their atomic arrangement to the child which, in turn, “looks” like its parents.

Mutation

Despite Crossover's best efforts, its usage alone does not maintain enough diversity in the pool of structures. Therefore, a very important set of candidates are introduced: the mutants. In Solids the two mutations implemented are known as lattice strain, and atomic exchange.

In lattice strain, the unit cell is deformed by applying a strain matrix to the cell's vectors and potentially modifying the interatomic distances of the candidate. This operator explores the energetic vicinity of the candidate and lets us explore it.

Atomic exchange is more aggressive since it takes the atomic positions and species of two different atoms inside the unit cell and swaps their species without changing the coordinates. This effectively creates spikes in the energy of the candidates but allows us to explore sections of the energy landscape that are impossible to reach using only Crossover or Lattice strain alone.

Relaxation and Selection of structures

Now, in nature, children's traits are tested against the environment to see if the inherited characteristics worked or not. Fit individuals are able to reproduce while inadequate traits are discarded. To check for fitness in theoretical chemistry, it is necessary to know if the child can lower the energy of its parents. This is achieved via local optimization, a process where theoretical calculations are used to modify certain parameters of the structure, such as unit cell and interatomic distances, to locate the most relaxed configuration of the child, i.e., the one with the lowest energy. Local optimization is achieved using external relaxation codes like VASP, Quantum Espresso, GULP and many others. Solids is interfaced with VASP, GULP and ASE's EMT. The image below displays a child's atomic arrangement before and after relaxation.

The resulting set of structures are ranked by energy and a fitness value is assigned to them. The value of the fitness is inversely proportional to the structure's energy, i.e., the lower the energy, the higher the fitness. Later, the candidates “compete” between them for the right to pass on their traits, but instead of fighting to the death, they are selected randomly, giving structures with higher values of fitness the greatest probability to be chosen. This has the effect of preferring adequate traits in new generations but keeping the mix rich in diversity.

Results

Finally, the whole process of generating candidates, relaxing them, and passing on their traits is repeated a given number of times. The final result is, hopefully, a putative Global Minimum, and a set of low-energy structures. Notice that we used the word putative next to global minimum, this is because it is near-impossible to evaluate all possible atomic configurations and there will always be a little possibility of finding a structure with lower energy. Although this might sound discouraging, it only means that no matter what, there is always room for improvement, and isn't that what science is all about?

Understanding the Output

The output of every search is divided into four categories: Generation of initial candidates, Niching, Generation summary, and Global summary wich are detailed in the next sections.

Generation of initial candidates

Since we're using the Evolutionary Algorithm, there is a zero (0) after GENERATION. This indicates that this is the first set of structures being created. As the search progresses, the value next to GENERATION increases to match the current iteration.

Below this section, the ID of each candidate describes how the structure was generated (random / crossover / mutant), the generation under study (e.g., _000_), and the structure number (e.g., _001_). Therefore, the fifth candidate created by crossover in generation 10 will have the ID crossover_010_005.

Next to the ID, you'll find the space group used to generate the structure (SG_). The numbering follows the Hermann–Mauguin convention, which classifies 3D space groups as triclinic (1–2), monoclinic (3–15), orthorhombic (16–74), tetragonal (75–142), trigonal–hexagonal (143–194), and cubic (195–230).

Naturally, the reference image will differ from yours due to the stochastic nature of the algorithm. However, you can control the number and type of structures generated by modifying the values of the flags nof_initpop (controls the initial population), nof_matings (controls the number of matings created), nof_strains (controls the number of mutants created by lattice strain), and nof_xchange (controls the number of mutants created by atomic exchange).

Niching

The niching process removes duplicate structural information by characterizing all individuals in a generation using MBTR descriptors. This is accomplished through Python's DScribe library, which computes the descriptors for each candidate. These fingerprints are then compared to one another, and any structures whose similarity exceeds a defined threshold are discarded. The flag controlling the similarity threshold tolerance is tol_similarity.

In the Evolutionary Algorithm, niching is applied in two stages: first, a comparison among elements within the current generation; and second, a comparison between the unique elements of that generation and all previously found structures (the Pool elements). Since structural relaxation may converge to the same minima, the first comparison removes redundant information within a given generation after optimization, while the second eliminates duplicates from the overall structure pool.

Finally, notice that there is an energy-based niching technique for the curated list of candidates. The energy of each structure in the set is compared to that of the lowest-energy candidate, and any candidates whose energy difference exceeds the threshold defined by the cutoff_energy flag are discarded.

Generation Summary

The entire compendium of structures found in the generation is displayed in the GEN. SUMMARY section. Here, you will find (in order from left-to-right) the ranking, ID, computed energy (in eV), energy difference relative to the lowest-energy structure found, and the fitness of each candidate. The fitness correlates with the probability of being chosen to participate in the inheritance process, and the structures are sorted by energy.

Global Summary

Finally, the GLOBAL SUMMARY section displays the best structures found so far through the entire run. The cutoff_population flag controls the number of structures presented in this list, and the candidates listed here compete to create the next set of crossover and mutants. Here, you will find (in order from left-to-right) the ranking, ID, computed energy (in eV), energy difference relative to the lowest-energy structure found, and the fitness of each candidate. The fitness correlates with the probability of being chosen to participate in the inheritance process, and the structures are sorted by energy.

Launching Solids

Once installed, in the working directory you will find the folder named Solids1.0/ and within it, the next executables:

  • run_solids_emt.py
  • run_solids_gulp.py
  • run_solids_vasp.py

Each of these codes contains the basic input required to run a search using a specific calculator—as written in the name of the code. We recommend first running a search using the default values for the EMT calculator. This is done by simply typing:

Copy 

            ./run_solids_emt.py

Calculations using EMT are extremely cheap, and can be carried out locally with fairly accurate results (as long as one uses the elements in which the potentials were trained, namely Al, Cu, Ag, Au, Ni, Pd and Pt). If you desire to personalize the values of the flags used in the search, just edit the information of the input_text located at the top of the run_solids_emt.py file using any desired/available text editor. A complete description of the flags used can be found in the section The Stochastich Method and EMT. This procedure is standard to all runs: edit the input_txt section inside the run_solids file that has the extension of the desired calculator, and execute them.

Understanding the Output

The output of every search is shown on the screen and it is divided into four categories: Generation of Candidates, Niching, Generation Summary and Global Summary. The information displayed in these sections will vary when using the different algorithms available in Solids (Stochastic and Evolutive), but their format is the same.

Generation of candidates

When using the Stochastic algorithm (SA), candidate structures are constructed only one time whereas when using the Evolutive algorithm (EA), this is an iterative process. Therefore, when using SA a list displaying all generated structures is shown:

In contrast, when using EA, this section looks like this:

Notice that there is a zero (0) after GENERATION. This is because this is the first set of structures to be created. However, the value next to GENERATION is updated in each iteration.

Below this section, you can find descriptions of the point group used to build the structure and the assigned ID number. In EA,descriptions include the type of operator used to create the new structures (mating and mutant). Of course, this image will differ from the one in your output because of the stochastic nature of the algorithms. The number of structures generated obeys the values assigned to nof_initpop, nof_matings, nof_strains, and nof_xchange values.

Niching

The niching process eliminates duplicate structural information by comparing MBTR descriptors of the candidates and eliminating those that are too alike. The parameter controlling the tolerance for similarity is tol_similarity. Also, Solids implements an energy cutoff that removes solutions whose energy difference, compared with the best solution so-far-located, exceeds a given threshold (cutoff_energy).

In EA, niching is implemented in two ways: Elements of a generation compared with their peers, and elements of the Pool compared with the generation. The first comparison removes redundancies in the generation, and the second one prevents redundancies in the pool of structures. Notice that energy-based niching is also carried out.

In SA, there are only generation and energy comparisons.

Generation Summary

The entire compendium of structures found in the run, along with their energies and energy differences relative to the lowest-energy structure, is displayed in the GEN. SUMMARY section. The resulting set of structures will consist of those unique candidates that possess adequate levels of energy. The display varies when using SA and EA, but the format is very similar. When using SA, the generation summary is displayed in stages. The first row is the number of candidates, followed by the ID of the candidate, its energy and the energy difference with respect to the local minimum. Notice that the ID contains information regarding the stage of the process and the number of candidates, e.g., stage_002_0001 corresponds to the best structure located in the second stage of the process.

Similarly, when using EA, the information is displayed by generation. Here, the ID contains information of the type of operator applied in the construction of the candidate, the generation it was built, and the number of structure, e.g., mutant_002_0003 implies that this candidate was built using mutation in the second generation and was the 3rd mutant created in that generation.

Global Summary

Finally, the GLOBAL SUMMARY section displays the best structures found through the entire run. The cutoff_population flag controls the number of structures presented in this list. When using EA, the structures listed are used to generate the next set of candidates, and are sorted by energy, with the lowest-energy candidates first in the list. Their energy difference relative to the lowest-energy structure found and their aptitude are presented in the last two rows.

Explorations using Solids

Solids and EMT, Al10 case

Editing the input

Before running any exploration, you need to edit the running_solids_emt.py file, which contains the default values for all required flags. This default input is set up to explore 3D Al10 crystals using the Stochastic Algorithm, which generates 50 random initial candidates and performs a two-stage relaxation process with EMT. However, you can modify any of these parameters by using your preferred text editor. The default values are:

Copy 

            #Construction of the initial population
            #This composition is used to build all structures
            ---COMPOSITION---
            Al    10
            ---COMPOSITION---
            formula_units 1 #Atoms in composition are multiplied by this number
            dimension 3 #Solids can handle 2- and 3-dimensional crystals
            volume_factor 1.0 #The volume factor of the unit cell
            tol_atomic_overlap 0.95 #The tolerances provided to allow atomic overlap
            nof_initpop 50 #The size of the initial population
            algorithm stochastic # Choose between stochastic / evolutive
            #Niching process
            tol_similarity 0.97 #Tolerance threshold for similarity
            cutoff_energy 5.0 #Energy Cut-off in eV
            cutoff_population 10 #Number of final candidates
            #Calculator
            calculator EMT #Available calculators: EMT, VASP, GULP
            nof_processes 5 #Number of parallel local opts
            #Evolutive algorithm parameters
            nof_matings 15 #Number of matings
            nof_strains 5 #Number of strains
            nof_xchange 0 #Number of atom exchanges
            #Halting criterion parameters
            #Stochastic algorithm
            nof_stages 2 #Number of Optimization Stages
            #Evolutive algorithm
            nof_repeats 10 #Stops if the best solutions are found 5 consecutive times
            nof_stagnant 5 #Controls the number of best solutions of nof_stagnant
            nof_generations 20 #Number of generations

You may have noticed that some flags include the labels Stochastic and Evolutionary at the top. These flags control the specific parameters required by each algorithm. If no label is specified, it means that both algorithms use the same flags. If you don’t mind the extra lines, you can keep all flags in the input file—Solids is designed to read only the case-sensitive flags relevant to the selected algorithm. To switch between algorithms, simply modify the value of the algorithm flag (and any associated ones), then run the code!

Running Solids

To run Solids and store the output in a file named “log.txt”simply type in:

Copy 

                ./run_solids_emt.py > log.txt

Expected results

The resulting structure is a Copper-structured crystal with the cubic Fm̅3m space group. Al is bonded to twelve equivalent Al atoms to form a mixture of corner, edge, and face-sharing AlAl12 cuboctahedra. All Al-Al bond lengths should be around 2.86 Å. The resulting structure is shown in the figure.

Since we used the Stochastich Algorithm for this search, this structure along with the rest found during the run are stored in the stage.vasp file. Since this is a two-stage run there will be a stage1.vasp and a stage2.vasp file, each containing the structural information of the structures in POSCAR format and sorted by energy, with lower energy-structures are on top. Similarly, when using the Evolutive Algorithm, the resulting structures are stored in the summary.vasp file.

Solids and GULP, the TiO2 case

Editing the input

Before running any exploration, you need to edit the running_solids_gulp.py file, which contains the default values for all required flags. This default input is set up to explore 3D TiO2 crystals using the Evolutive Algorithm, which generates 10 random initial candidates, and improves upon them through 10 generations, each composed by 20 matings and 12 mutants. The relaxation code is GULP. You can modify any of these parameters by using your preferred text editor. The default values are:

Copy 

            #Construction of the initial population
            #This composition is used to build all structures
            ---COMPOSITION---
            Ti    1
            O     2
            ---COMPOSITION---
            formula_units 4 #Atoms in composition are multiplied by this number
            dimension 3 #Solids can handle 2- and 3-dimensional crystals
            volume_factor 1.0 #The volume factor of the unit cell
            tol_atomic_overlap 0.95 #The tolerances provided to allow atomic overlap
            nof_initpop 10 #The size of the initial population
            algorithm evolutive # Choose between stochastic / evolutive
            #Niching process
            tol_similarity 0.97 #Tolerance threshold for similarity
            cutoff_energy 5.0 #Energy Cut-off in eV
            cutoff_population 10 #Number of final candidates
            #Calculator
            calculator GULP #Available calculators: EMT, VASP, GULP
            nof_processes 5 #Number of parallel local opts
            path_exe /installdir/gulp #Path to the executable of GULP
            #Evolutive algorithm parameters
            nof_matings 20 #Number of matings
            nof_strains 6 #Number of strains
            nof_xchange 6 #Number of atom exchanges
            #Halting criterion parameters
            #Stochastic algorithm
            nof_stages 2 #Number of Optimization Stages
            #Evolutive algorithm
            nof_repeats 10 #Stops if the best solutions are found 5 consecutive times
            nof_stagnant 5 #Controls the number of best solutions of nof_stagnant
            nof_generations 10 #Number of generations
            ---GULP---
            opti conj conp
            switch_minimiser bfgs gnorm 0.5
            vectors
            LATTICEVECTORS
            frac
            COORDINATES
            lib /Gulp/Libraries/matsui-akaogi.lib
            ---GULP---

The ---GULP--- section comprises all the required flags to run a calculation using GULP and the sudo potentials for the TiO2. The flags LATTICEVECTORS and COORDINATES are automatically replaced by Solids to match the ones of the structure at hand. GULP uses a combination of Buckingham and Lennard–Jones potentials to describe the interactions of the system, and these values are specific for the TiO2. NOTE: If you change the composition of the crystal, this section must change as well. Sudopotentials are highly case-sensitive and are not interchangeable. On the other hand, you may have noticed that some flags include the labels Stochastic and Evolutionary at the top. These flags control the specific parameters required by each algorithm. If no label is specified, it means that both algorithms use the same flags. If you don't mind the extra lines, you can keep all flags in the input file —Solids is designed to read only the case-sensitive flags relevant to the selected algorithm. To switch between algorithms, simply modify the value of the algorithm flag (and any associated ones), then run the code!

Expected results

The resulting structure is the well-known rutile phase with the tetragonal P4₂/mnm space group where the Ti4+ (blue) is bonded to six equivalent O2-(red) atoms to form a mixture of corner and edge-sharing TiO6 octahedra. There are four shorter (1.95 Å) and two longer (1.98 Å) Ti-O bond lengths. The resulting structure is shown in the figure.

Since the exploration uses the Evolutive algorithm, the best structures are stored in the summary.vasp file along with the higher energy structures located during the run. In the figure, we can see the Anatase phase (A) and the Rutile phase (B) since it is very likely that, depending on the initial population size, the anatase phase is also present in the higher-energy allotropes list.
